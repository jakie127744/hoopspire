BERLIN, September 13, 2026

France spent the opening 16 minutes of the FIBA Women's Basketball World Cup final making the United States look unusually uncomfortable.

Then the game changed.

The United States erased a 14-point deficit, entered halftime trailing France by only two, and then produced a 30-12 third quarter that transformed a potential French upset into a 97-79 American championship. The victory gave the United States its fifth consecutive World Cup title and 12th overall, while France earned its first medal in the competition in its first appearance in a World Cup final.

The most important number is obvious:

USA 30, France 12 in the third quarter.

But the third quarter was the result, not the entire explanation.

The more useful question is why France could build a 14-point lead, why that advantage had almost disappeared by halftime, and what allowed the Americans to turn a two-point deficit into a 16-point lead in only 10 minutes.

The answer lies in four things: France's early shot-making, the United States' ability to create additional possessions, the Americans' superior overall two-point efficiency, and a second-half defensive response that France could not answer.

## 1. How did France build the 14-point lead?

France did not stumble into its advantage.

It created it through efficient perimeter shooting and Gabby Williams' early offensive control.

The French went on a 21-2 run and opened a 14-point advantage at 31-17 early in the second quarter. Williams scored 16 of her 20 points before halftime, while France repeatedly found enough perimeter production to keep the American defense under pressure.

The shooting numbers explain the initial problem for the United States.

France finished the game 11-for-24 from three-point range, 45.8%, compared with 8-for-19, 42.1%, for the Americans.

That is important because France was not merely scoring through difficult individual plays. Its early perimeter accuracy made the floor more difficult for the American defense to compress.

The United States had to respect the three-point line.

That gave France room to attack.

Dominique Malonga supplied the interior scoring, finishing with 21 points on 8-for-12 shooting, while Williams combined scoring and playmaking with 20 points and seven assists.

The result was an offense that could threaten the United States in more than one way.

And then France's defense added another problem.

The French led 20-17 after the opening quarter and opened the second quarter with 11 consecutive points, reaching 31-17.

For roughly a quarter and a half, France had established both the scoreboard advantage and the rhythm.

But the lead was beginning to disappear before halftime.

## 2. Why didn't the 14-point lead survive halftime?

The United States did not need to completely reverse the game before the break.

It only needed to stop the damage.

That distinction became important.

The Americans scored 26 points in the second quarter after scoring only 17 in the first. France still led 45-43 at halftime, but the entire complexion of the final had changed.

Caitlin Clark's three-pointer at the buzzer reduced the deficit to two.

That shot mattered less because it was worth three points than because of what it prevented.

France had built a 14-point lead.

Instead of taking that advantage into the locker room, it entered halftime protecting a two-point margin.

The psychological interpretation should not be overstated. What can be established is the statistical and game-state effect: France's 14-point advantage had been reduced to two in the span of roughly one quarter.

That gave the United States a completely different starting point for the second half.

And then Stewart immediately attacked.

The United States opened the third quarter with an 11-2 run, with Stewart contributing to the initial response. The Americans moved from 45-43 down to 54-47 ahead.

The comeback was no longer theoretical.

The United States had taken the game back.

## 3. What specifically changed in the third quarter?

This is where the final was decided.

The United States scored 30 points.

France scored 12.

That 18-point difference turned a two-point halftime deficit into a 16-point American lead entering the fourth quarter.

The turning point was not one player suddenly scoring 20 points.

It was a sequence of efficient possessions in which the Americans combined interior scoring, perimeter shooting and additional possessions.

The clearest documented sequence came after Stewart helped level the game.

Kahleah Copper and Jackie Young then hit consecutive three-pointers, pushing the United States to a 58-51 lead. Later, Clark hit another three that stretched the advantage to 19 early in the fourth quarter.

That sequence is important because it shows what France was suddenly being asked to defend.

The Americans could attack inside.

They could create perimeter shots.

And when France needed to respond offensively, it no longer had the same shooting rhythm that had produced the early lead.

Young was especially important.

She finished with 18 points, six rebounds and five assists, including four made three-pointers.

That was more than a scoring contribution.

It meant France had another perimeter scorer to account for while Stewart remained an interior threat.

The American offense therefore did not depend on one answer.

France had to solve several.

The possession battle explains why the shooting difference was not enough

This is where the box score becomes more revealing.

The United States attempted 64 field goals to France's 54 and collected 12 offensive rebounds to France's six. The Americans also committed 15 turnovers to France's 16.

Those numbers matter because France actually had the better three-point percentage.

If the game were simply about three-point shooting, France's 45.8% should have kept the contest much closer.

It didn't.

The United States created more opportunities to score.

Using the standard estimated-possession formula:

Possessions ≈ FGA + 0.44 × FTA − offensive rebounds + turnovers

the final box-score numbers produce approximately:

USA: 78 estimated possessions

France: 73 estimated possessions

That is roughly five additional possessions for the Americans.

Five possessions do not automatically explain an 18-point victory. But they give context to the larger statistical difference: the United States combined more opportunities with better overall shooting efficiency.

Its effective field-goal percentage was approximately 62.5%, compared with approximately 60.6% for France.

The difference is modest.

The possession advantage was not.

And the offensive-rebounding difference was especially striking.

Using offensive rebounds divided by offensive rebounds plus the opponent's defensive rebounds, the estimated offensive-rebound rates were approximately:

USA: 38.7%

France: 18.8%

That is a major difference in the number of missed shots that were converted into another American opportunity.

It is also why Stewart's 10 rebounds matter beyond her double-double. Five were offensive rebounds.

The United States was not simply finishing possessions better.

It was extending them.

## 4. Why did France's offense collapse to 12 points?

This is the most important unanswered question from the final.

France scored 45 points in the first half.

It scored only 12 in the third quarter.

That cannot be explained simply by saying that France "went cold."

France's final three-point percentage was actually better than America's.

The issue was that its early offensive success did not survive the change in game state.

Williams, who had scored 16 points before halftime, finished with 20. That means she scored only four points after the break. Malonga remained France's leading scorer with 21, but the French offense no longer produced the same collective output.

The American defense also produced seven steals to France's five, while France committed 16 turnovers to 15 for the United States.

Those numbers by themselves do not prove that every French turnover was forced by American pressure.

But combined with the dramatic third-quarter scoring split, they demonstrate that France was no longer converting possessions into points at anything close to its first-half rate.

That is the crucial distinction.

The United States did not need to make France completely ineffective.

It only needed to prevent France from continuing to score at the rate that had created the 14-point lead.

Once France's offensive efficiency fell, the American advantages in shooting efficiency, offensive rebounding and depth became much more consequential.

The Four Factors tell a surprisingly specific story

The final box score produces an interesting Four Factors profile.

Four Factor	USA	France	What it tells us
Effective FG%	~62.5%	~60.6%	USA had a modest overall shooting-efficiency edge
Turnover rate*	~16.7%	~20.3%	France surrendered possessions at a higher rate
Offensive rebound rate*	~38.7%	~18.8%	USA created a major second-chance advantage
Free-throw rate*	39.1%	48.1%	France generated more free throws relative to field-goal attempts

*Calculated from the available final box-score figures using standard formulas; provider definitions can vary slightly.

This table changes the interpretation of the game.

France actually won one important possession-efficiency battle: it generated more free throws relative to its field-goal attempts.

Its 25 free-throw attempts were identical to America's.

It also shot better from three.

Yet the United States still won by 18.

Why?

Because the Americans combined a small overall shooting-efficiency advantage with a much larger offensive-rebounding advantage and a lower turnover rate.

That is the statistical fingerprint of the comeback.

The offensive glass became increasingly expensive for France

There is an important basketball mechanism behind those numbers.

An offensive rebound does not merely add a rebound to a player's statistical line.

It can erase the defensive team's successful work on the previous possession.

France can force a miss.

If the United States retrieves that miss, the defensive possession continues.

That is particularly valuable in a championship game because it gives the offense another chance to attack before France can reset its defense.

The United States finished with 12 offensive rebounds against six for France.

That six-rebound difference does not translate directly into six additional baskets.

But it represents a substantial difference in possession extension.

And the Americans were efficient enough to turn those additional opportunities into points.

The shooting story is more complicated than "USA shot better"

The final field-goal numbers were:

USA: 36-for-64, 56.3%

France: 26-for-54, 48.1%

FIBA's game page also records a substantial American advantage inside the arc:

USA: 62.2% on two-point shots

France: 50.0%.

That is arguably more important to the story than the three-point numbers.

France made 11 threes.

The United States made only eight.

Yet the Americans made 13 more field goals overall.

That means the United States was winning the game primarily through two-point efficiency and volume of possessions, not through a three-point barrage.

The Americans made 28 of 45 two-point attempts.

France made 15 of 30.

That is a difference of 13 made two-point baskets.

It is difficult to overcome that kind of interior efficiency simply by making three-pointers at a slightly higher rate.

Stewart was the stabilizer, not merely the leading scorer

Breanna Stewart finished with 22 points, 10 rebounds and an efficiency rating of 30, earning her second World Cup MVP award.

Her importance becomes clearer when placed inside the game structure.

France's early shooting forced the American defense to respect the perimeter.

The United States needed someone capable of producing efficient offense without requiring the game to become a three-point contest.

Stewart provided that.

She shot 7-for-10 from the field and 8-for-9 from the free-throw line.

That efficiency gave the Americans a reliable source of points while the game was still being stabilized.

Young then supplied the perimeter counter.

Copper supplied another scoring threat.

Clark contributed 12 points on 4-for-5 shooting, including 2-for-3 from three, despite playing only 13 minutes.

The important point is not that every American player dominated.

It is that France could not eliminate every American option simultaneously.

Clark's biggest contribution may have been timing

Clark's 12 points are modest beside Stewart's 22.

But two of her baskets were particularly important to the game's narrative.

Her three-pointer at halftime cut France's lead to 45-43.

Later, another three helped extend the American lead to 19 at the beginning of the fourth quarter. Reuters specifically identified that shot as part of the sequence that put the game beyond France's immediate reach.

That is why raw scoring totals can be misleading in a game like this.

Clark did not need to dominate the final.

Her most important shots came at moments when the value of changing the scoreboard was greater than it would have been during an ordinary possession.

France's collapse was real, but its first half was real too

It would be easy to turn the final into a simple story of American superiority.

The numbers do not support that simplification.

France led by 14.

It led 45-43 at halftime.

It shot 45.8% from three.

Williams and Malonga combined for 41 points.

The French were not overwhelmed for 40 minutes.

They were overwhelmed for one decisive stretch.

That distinction matters.

The third quarter produced an 18-point swing.

The other three quarters combined for a four-point USA advantage:

USA outscored France 17-20 in the first, 26-25 in the second and 24-22 in the fourth.

That means the championship was effectively decided by the 30-12 third quarter.

This was not a 40-minute demolition.

It was a game in which one team produced a championship-level response at precisely the point when the other team could not match it.

The key moments

1. France reaches 31-17

The 11-0 start to the second quarter extended the French lead to 14. France's perimeter shooting and Williams' scoring were driving the game.

2. Clark's halftime three

The Americans enter halftime down only 45-43 instead of carrying a double-digit deficit into the break.

3. Stewart helps ignite the third-quarter response

The United States opens the second half aggressively and quickly takes the lead.

4. Copper and Young hit consecutive threes

The United States moves ahead 58-51, converting the comeback into control.

5. The third quarter ends 30-12

USA leads 73-57.

At that point, France needs both stops and efficient offense to recover.

It gets neither consistently enough.

6. Clark's fourth-quarter three

The American lead reaches 19, effectively ending the possibility of another French comeback.

What did the coaches have to solve?

For France, the problem changed during the game.

Early on, the French offense was forcing the United States to respect its perimeter shooting while Williams and Malonga provided additional creation.

After halftime, France had to respond to an American offense that was simultaneously shooting efficiently inside the arc, extending possessions on the offensive glass and receiving perimeter production from Young, Copper and Clark.

That creates a difficult defensive tradeoff.

Commit more help toward the interior and the Americans have more room outside.

Stay attached to the perimeter and Stewart and the American interior attack have more space.

The available statistics establish the outcome of that problem. They do not, by themselves, establish every specific defensive instruction France used on every possession. That distinction matters.

The same caution applies to the American defense.

We can establish that France fell from 45 first-half points to 12 in the third.

We can establish that Williams scored only four points after halftime.

We can establish that France committed 16 turnovers overall and finished with 15 assists.

But without a complete film-coded defensive possession database, we should not claim that one particular defensive coverage caused every French breakdown.

The evidence supports a broader conclusion:

The United States disrupted enough of France's offensive efficiency after halftime that France could no longer trade baskets while the Americans were simultaneously producing efficient offense.

What the game teaches

The final provides a useful lesson in why a basketball game cannot be explained by the three-point line alone.

France shot better from three.

France attempted the same number of free throws.

France had a 14-point lead.

And France still lost by 18.

The United States won because it combined several smaller advantages into one overwhelming stretch:

better two-point shooting, more field-goal attempts, more offensive rebounds, a lower turnover rate, multiple reliable scorers and a dramatic defensive improvement after halftime.

None of those factors alone explains the final.

Together, they do.

The numbers also explain why the comeback felt so sudden.

At halftime, France was still ahead.

Ten minutes later, the French were down 16.

The decisive change was not simply that the United States started making shots.

It was that the Americans began winning the possessions around those shots.

That is what turned a 14-point deficit into a 97-79 championship.

The final picture

The United States finished the night shooting 56.3% from the field, 42.1% from three and 68% from the line. France shot 48.1%, 45.8% and 64%, respectively.

The Americans had 21 assists to France's 15, 15 turnovers to France's 16, and a substantial advantage on the offensive glass.

Stewart supplied 22 points and 10 rebounds.

Young supplied 18 points, six rebounds and five assists.

Williams and Malonga combined for 41 points for France.

And the scoreboard ultimately came down to one extraordinary quarter:

USA 30, France 12.

France had the breakthrough first.

The United States had the response.

And in a World Cup final, the response was the part that mattered most.

The United States leaves Berlin with its 12th World Cup title and fifth consecutive championship. France leaves with its first World Cup silver medal and the knowledge that it had the reigning champion eight minutes away from a very different ending.

The final was not decided by the team that avoided adversity.

It was decided by the team that changed the game after adversity arrived.
